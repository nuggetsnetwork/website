Babylon, Iraq

Built: 2026-11-01. Range: 1894 BCE to 224. Opens at: 575 BCE. Chapter 28.

Babylon is the second chapter for Iraq, after Baghdad. It covers a different age (the cuneiform kings, 1894 BCE to 224) and adds eight houses.

Chronology followed

Dates are the middle chronology, as printed on Wikipedia's List of Babylonian kings. Other chronologies move everything before about 600 BCE by decades. Every date before about 1000 BCE is approximate, and the blurbs and captions say so. Years before the common era are shown as BCE.

Not verified

  • Dates: the Kassite kings Agum II and Kurigalzu I are undated on the list read; their reigns here (1594 to 1531 and 1400 to 1375 BCE) are placements, not records. Karaindash's end is set to the year before Kurigalzu I's placement. The Second Sealand, Bazi and Elamite kings are a few years each.
  • The Parthian kings' parentage beyond what the list gives; Orodes II and Phraates IV chosen for the line, not for any event read.
  • Monument years: every year except the Ishtar Gate (569 BCE, from its page) is a placement inside a reign. The Processional Way pin is on its route between the gate and the palace, by eye. Nebuchadnezzar II's Esagila work, the Etemenanki rebuilding and the Southern Palace are in the pages, but not their years.
  • The Greek theatre's attribution to Alexander rests on a search summary of a Livius entry, not a page read; the 323 BCE year is a placeholder.
  • Origins, routes and every ring: from memory and by eye. The Achaemenid and Seleucid rings are copies of the coarse Persepolis rings. The Amorite steppe origin, the Zagros origin of the Kassites (the page says most scholars link them to the Zagros), Isin, the Chaldean south and the Parthian home at Nisa are not from a page read.
  • Seleucia on the Tigris, Susa, Ecbatana, Uruk, Mari and Nippur coordinates on routes: from memory.

Exclusions

  • The Sasanians (224 to 651) and later: the chapter stops where the list ends, with Artabanus IV in 224. The Sasanians held Babylonia, but no page read says anything about Babylon under them, and they ruled from Ctesiphon. A longer chapter would add them, and would join the Persepolis Sasanian house to a group in the links file. The abandonment of Babylon (about the 11th century on the page read) is after any sovereign who ruled from it.
  • The First Sealand dynasty (about 1725 to 1475 BCE): a rival kingdom in the south, not a house that held Babylon.
  • The Kassite kings before Burnaburiash I other than Agum II and Kurigalzu I's successors not named: dropped to keep to about twelve.
  • Rebel kings of Babylon: Nebuchadnezzar III and IV (522 and 521 BCE), Shamash-eriba and Bel-shimanni (484), Nidin-Bel: held the city for months and not as houses; and Bardiya, Xerxes II and Sogdianus, Artaxerxes IV, Seleucus III, Antiochus V, Timarchus, Antiochus VII and the Characene king Hyspaosines: under about 15 years or contested, folded away.
  • Philip III Arrhidaeus (323 to 317 BCE) and Alexander IV: left out of the Macedonian house; this leaves a gap of about five years between Alexander and Antigonus that is an omission, not history.
  • Seleucus III is skipped in the Seleucid line (his brother follows), Antiochus V, and the Parthian kings between Phraates II and Mithridates II, and Phraates IV and Artabanus II, and Artabanus II and Vologases I: curation. Parthian rival kings and queens listed on the page are not kept.
  • Belshazzar and Nitocris (Nabonidus's coruler and mother, per the list) are mentioned, not kept as rulers.
  • Ziggurat of Borsippa (Birs Nimrud, about 18 km south-west, Nebuchadnezzar II's rebuilding of Nabu's temple): researched and written, then dropped because at that distance the builder's map for Nebuchadnezzar zoomed out until the seven pins in Babylon itself collided. It still stands about 52 metres high and is worth a later chapter note or a ruler of its own.
  • Monuments left out: the Greek-period and Parthian-period buildings other than the theatre, the Median Wall, Nebuchadnezzar's North and Summer Palaces (named on the Nebuchadnezzar page, no coordinates), the walls of Babylon and the modern Saddam Hill and museum; the Gate of Ishtar's reconstruction is only described. No monument has been added for the Achaemenid, Second Isin, Sealand or Parthian houses because no page read gives one with a location.

Judgement calls

  • The Second Isin, Second Sealand, Bazi, Elamite and E dynasties and their Chaldean chiefs are one house, "The native dynasties after the Kassites", dated 1154 to 733 BCE, because each held the city too briefly to stand alone and the alternative was a 400-year gap; the lineage tree has several roots and the real gaps between kings stay visible.
  • The Assyrian-ruled period is one house of Assyrian kings, Chaldean rebels (Nabu-mukin-zeri, Marduk-apla-iddina II) and the vassal Kandalanu; Sennacherib's first, second and Aššur-nādin-šumi reigns are folded into one; Ashurbanipal's first reign is folded and his second set at 648 to 647 because the list overlaps him with Kandalanu.
  • Hand-offs follow the one-year rule and shift the list's dates by one year: post-Kassite from 1154 (list 1153), Achaemenid from 538 (539), Seleucus I from 311 (312 on his page, 305 on the list), Antiochus I from 280, Demetrius II to 141, Parthians from 140 (141). Labashi-Marduk is a one-year reign at 556 beside Nabonidus.
  • The Lion of Babylon stands under Samsu-Ditana at 1595 BCE because that is the date the page gives, though the page also says most experts think it Hittite and the date conflicts with another line on the same page ("over 2,600 years old").
  • Achaemenid and Macedonian-Seleucid houses are the same houses as in Persepolis and joined to it in the links file, but with different ruler lists: here the Achaemenids are the kings who held Babylon (Cyrus from 538), and the Macedonian house is Alexander, Antigonus and the Seleucids with no Peucestas.
  • The Parthian house has no monument and the chapter ends with the last Parthian king in Babylonia; the Seleucid and Parthian kings did not rule from Babylon (Seleucia, then Ctesiphon).
  • The Hanging Gardens appears only as a traditional site: the pin is a visitor marker, and the blurb says the gardens were never found.
  • Dur-Kurigalzu (about 100 km north) is counted as Babylon's reach, as Persepolis counted Naqsh-e Rustam; it sits alone under Kurigalzu I so the builder's map for that king is the only one it widens.
  • The Euphrates drawn on the plan is the Hillah branch from OpenStreetMap, which passes beside the ruins as the ancient river did; the main river flows farther west today.
